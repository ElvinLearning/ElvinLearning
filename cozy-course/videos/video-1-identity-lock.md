# Video 1 — Identity Lock
### Building a character whose face survives twelve shots
**Target runtime: 20 minutes · Screen recording + both presenters on camera**

---

## The one job of this video

The viewer watches a face get locked down, then watches that same face hold across three completely
different shots. If they see that happen live, they believe the whole system. If they don't, no
amount of explanation saves it.

**Everything else is decoration. Protect the payoff at 14:00.**

---

## Before you hit record

- [ ] Identity kit already shot and organized in folders (do NOT film the photoshoot — reference it)
- [ ] Generation tool open and logged in, credits confirmed
- [ ] A **pre-generated backup** of every result you plan to show live, in a folder on the desktop
- [ ] Browser: close every other tab, clear notifications, hide the bookmarks bar
- [ ] Screen at 1920×1080, UI zoomed to ~125% so text is readable on a phone
- [ ] Both presenters have Book Two open to the shot list page

> **The backup rule.** Live generation fails, queues stall, and a model has a bad day. Have every
> result pre-generated. If something is slow, say "these take about ninety seconds, here's one I ran
> this morning" and cut to it. Nobody minds. Everybody minds four minutes of a loading spinner.

---

## Run of show

### 00:00 – 01:30 · Cold open: show the problem
**On camera, both of you. No intro, no logo, no "welcome back."**

Open on a screen recording already playing: four clips of the "same" character generated the normal
way, cut back to back. The face is different in every one.

> **Quincy:** "That's supposed to be one person. Four shots, four different faces. This is the thing
> that stops people from making anything longer than eight seconds with AI, and it's not the model's
> fault — it's a workflow problem. In the next twenty minutes we're going to fix it permanently."

**Kayson:** name what's coming — "By the end of this video you'll watch the same face hold across
three completely different shots, and you'll know exactly how to do it."

*No branding yet. It goes at the end.*

---

### 01:30 – 04:00 · The identity kit, on screen
**Screen share: the actual folder structure.**

Walk the folders: `face/`, `expression/`, `body/`, `wardrobe/`, `voice/`. Open a few images.

Hit these points, in this order:
1. **Seven angles**, and open the profile shot specifically — "this one is why profile shots work later."
2. **Expressions matter** because emotion changes face shape.
3. **Wardrobe folders repeat the angles** — "this is why the jacket doesn't change between cuts."
4. **Flat even light, plain wall, no filters, no portrait mode.** Say *no portrait mode* out loud; a lot of people get this wrong.

> **Kayson:** "This took forty-five minutes to shoot one time. We've used it in every video since."

**On screen:** the printable shot list page from Book Two. Tell them it's in the PDF.

---

### 04:00 – 06:30 · The rule most people get wrong
**Screen share: side-by-side comparison.**

This is the single most valuable two minutes in the video. Show a profile shot generated two ways:

| Left | Right |
|---|---|
| Conditioned on front-facing refs only | Conditioned on the profile refs |
| Model invents a profile — wrong face | Holds |

> **Quincy:** "Same prompt. Same model. The only difference is which reference images we handed it.
> Most of what people call 'the model drifting' is this."

State the rule plainly and let it land: **match the reference angle to the shot angle.**

---

### 06:30 – 09:00 · Set up shot one
**Screen share: the tool, live.**

Build one prompt using the house formula from Book Two, narrating each slot as you type it:

`[SUBJECT] · [ACTION] · [CAMERA] · [SETTING + LIGHT] · [REALISM BLOCK] · [NEGATIVE BLOCK]`

Say why each slot exists as you fill it. Two specific callouts:
- **"One action."** — "Not walks in, sits down, and speaks. One thing."
- **"One camera move."** — "Slow push in. That's it."

Then attach the references — and say out loud which ones and why: *"3/4 left, because that's the
angle of this shot."*

Hit generate. Set expectations: "about ninety seconds."

---

### 09:00 – 11:00 · Three candidates, not one
**While it runs — talk over it, don't wait in silence.**

> **Quincy:** "We generate three of every shot. Always. Not because we're indecisive — because one
> take means you either accept what you got or pay again. Three means you choose."

Cover:
- Cost of three candidates vs. cost of prompt-spinning (Book Two has the math)
- Photographers don't take one frame
- **The real waste** is generating one clip twelve times while tweaking words

Bring in the result. Show all three candidates side by side. **Pick one out loud and say why** —
that reasoning is the thing people can't get from a blog post.

---

### 11:00 – 14:00 · Shots two and three
**Screen share: build two more shots on the same identity.**

Deliberately vary them so the payoff lands harder:
- **Shot 2:** different angle (profile or 3/4 opposite) — use the matching refs, say so
- **Shot 3:** different setting and lighting entirely

Keep it brisk. You've already taught the formula; now you're demonstrating repeatability.

Cut to pre-generated results if the queue is slow. No apology, just keep moving.

---

### 14:00 – 16:00 · THE PAYOFF
**Full screen. Stop talking over it.**

Play all three shots back to back. Different angles, different lighting, different framing.
**Same person.**

Let it play once in silence.

> **Kayson:** "Three shots, three setups, one face. That's the whole thing."

Then play it a second time and point at the specific things holding: jawline, hairline, skin tone,
the wardrobe.

*This is the moment the video exists for. Do not rush it and do not talk through the first playthrough.*

---

### 16:00 – 18:30 · The six rules
**On camera, both of you. Book Two visible on screen as a list.**

Run the six rules from Book One, one line each, trading off between you:

1. Never mix identity versions inside one video
2. Match reference angle to shot angle
3. Match wardrobe refs to scene wardrobe
4. Never route a talking-to-camera shot through text-to-video
5. Once a take is approved, never regenerate it
6. Keep a notes file on what drifted where

Spend the most time on **4** and **5**.

On 4: "We're not covering the talking-head route in this video — that's video two — but know now
that generating a talking close-up from text is the fastest way to look cheap."

On 5: "'Let me just run one more to see' has cost people entire days. Locked is locked."

---

### 18:30 – 20:00 · Close and hand off
**On camera.**

> **Quincy:** "You now have a character you can put anywhere. Video two takes this identity and
> builds a complete video with it — shot list, generation, picking takes, and the edit in CapCut,
> start to finish, nothing cut out."

Assign homework and be specific:
- Shoot your own identity kit using the printable list in Book Two
- Generate **one shot, three candidates**
- Then generate the **same shot at a different angle** with the matching references

> "Do that before video two. It's forty-five minutes and it's the step everybody skips."

**Now** the Cozy Digital end card. First and only branding in the video.

## Things to say because they build trust

- "This took us a while to figure out and we wasted a lot of money before we did."
- "That one's not great actually — here's why we'd re-roll it." *(Show a bad take on purpose. Nothing builds credibility faster than showing a failure and diagnosing it.)*
- "Prices change constantly, so check current rates — the routing logic is what stays true."

## Things not to say

- Anything that dates the video hard to one tool version
- Guaranteed results or income claims
- Trash talk about specific companies — critique the *workflow*, not the brand
